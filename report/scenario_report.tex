\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note builds three scenarios for the Canadian economy over 2026Q3--2028Q3 and maps
them into paths for real GDP, unemployment, CPI inflation, the Bank of Canada policy rate and new
house prices. The scenarios differ in two external drivers that Canada does not control: the WTI oil
price and US real GDP growth. A vector autoregression (VAR) with these drivers as exogenous variables,
estimated on 2009Q3--2026Q2 data, translates each driver path into Canadian outcomes. In the downside
scenario, a US slowdown with oil falling to \$50 calibrated to the 2001 US recession, Canadian GDP
contracts for three consecutive quarters, unemployment peaks near 7.9\%, and the policy rate falls close
to its effective lower bound by mid-2028. All code and data are available at
\url{https://github.com/Angelgonzales7067/Canada_macro_scenario}.
\end{abstract}

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Approach}

The exercise follows the workflow commonly used for bank scenario analysis and stress testing:
(1) write a short narrative for each scenario; (2) translate it into paths for a few external drivers;
(3) use a model estimated on history to map the drivers into domestic variables; and (4) apply
judgment where the model is a poor guide.

\subsection{Data}

The sample is quarterly, 2009Q3 to 2026Q2 (68 quarters). It starts with the first full quarter of the
Bank of Canada's daily policy-rate series (V39079, available from April 21, 2009) and ends with the
latest quarter for which Canadian and US GDP are published. Monthly series are averaged to quarters
before transformation. Growth rates are computed as annualized log differences, $400\,\Delta\ln x_t$;
the oil price enters as the quarterly log change, $100\,\Delta\ln P_t$.

\subsection{Model}

The model is a VAR with exogenous variables (VARX):
\begin{equation}
  y_t = c + A_1\, y_{t-1} + B_0\, x_t + B_1\, x_{t-1} + \Gamma\, d_t + u_t ,
\end{equation}
where $y_t$ contains the five Canadian variables, $x_t$ contains oil-price growth and US GDP growth,
and $d_t$ contains dummy variables for 2020Q2 and 2020Q3 that absorb the COVID-19 collapse and rebound.
Treating oil and US GDP as exogenous reflects the small-open-economy assumption: Canadian conditions do
not move world oil prices or US output within the quarter. It also makes scenario analysis direct,
because a scenario is simply a chosen path for $x_t$.

The lag length is one quarter due to lack of observations. The model is estimated by OLS equation by equation using
the R package \texttt{vars}.

\subsection{Scenario forecasts and judgment}

For each scenario, the oil and US GDP paths are fed into the estimated model and the Canadian variables
are forecast nine quarters ahead, conditional on those paths. The 2026Q3 oil price is set to its actual
average (\$87.12) in every scenario. For every scenario, the policy rate was imposed to be greater than or equal to 0.25\%

\textbf{Downside.} Canadian real GDP contracts for three consecutive quarters (2026Q4--2027Q2), with a
trough of $-1.0\%$ annualized in 2027Q1, and the level falls about 0.4\% from its peak. This is a mild
technical recession, similar in severity to Canada's experience in 2001. Unemployment rises steadily to a
peak of about 7.9\% in 2028Q2, 1.2 points above its 2026Q2 level of 6.7\%. Inflation dips below zero in
2027Q1 as lower oil prices pass through, and the policy rate is cut to about 0.3\% by 2028Q3, close
to its effective lower bound. New house prices decline through 2027.

\textbf{Upside.} Stronger US demand lifts Canadian GDP growth by about 0.4 points relative to the baseline
in 2027, and unemployment is slightly lower. Because the upside oil path declines more slowly than the
baseline path, average 2027 oil prices are similar in both, so the upside works almost entirely through
the US demand channel.

\textbf{Supply versus demand shocks.} During development, an alternative downside with oil rising to
\$110 (a supply shock) and US growth of 1.5\% was also run. It \emph{raised} Canadian GDP growth relative
to the baseline (by about 0.4 points in 2027) and pushed CPI inflation up by about 0.7 points, because
Canada is a net oil exporter and higher prices improve its terms of trade. For Canada, the more damaging
combination is a demand shock in which oil prices fall \emph{and} US growth weakens, which is why the
final downside takes that form. History supports this: in 2014--16 oil collapsed while the US kept
growing, and Canadian unemployment did not rise nationally, whereas in 2001 and 2008--09 US weakness
was accompanied by rising Canadian unemployment.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Linearity.} The VAR captures average historical relationships. It likely understates
        responses to large shocks; a 2008-sized shock would probably produce a milder outcome than the
        $-4.4\%$ GDP decline Canada actually experienced.
  \item \textbf{Short sample and imprecise estimates.} The policy-rate series limits the sample to
        68 quarters. In the GDP equation, only contemporaneous US growth is statistically significant
        at the 5\% level; oil coefficients are positive but imprecise.
  \item \textbf{Mean reversion.} Forecasts drift toward sample averages. Part of the rise in
        unemployment in every scenario, including the baseline, reflects reversion toward the
        2009--2026 average of about 7\%.
  \item \textbf{Missing channels.} The exchange rate, Canadian fiscal policy, and feedback from Canada
        to the world are not modelled. Forecast uncertainty bands are not reported.
  \item \textbf{Judgment.} The policy-rate floor is imposed by hand, and scenario probabilities are not
        assigned.
\end{itemize}
